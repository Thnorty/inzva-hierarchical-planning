Does planning at two time scales beat planning at one?

We train a small recurrent world model on PushT, then plan with it two ways: the standard method already in the repo, and our own coarse-to-fine method. The environment, the data, and the model stay fixed, so any difference in the results comes from the thing we changed.

01

Why we build on stable-worldmodel

stable-worldmodel is not a competitor to our idea. It is the test bench, and everything it gives us is work we do not have to redo:

  • A standard PushT environment, so our numbers are comparable with published work.
  • Data collection and loading, so we write no dataset code.
  • CEM and other planners, so our baseline is one we did not write and cannot accidentally cripple.
  • An evaluation loop that reports success rate.
  • Sixteen factors of variation for PushT, which let us test generalization with no extra setup.

Because all of that is shared and fixed, the comparison stays honest. That is the entire reason we are using it.

02

What two time scales means

Time scale means how far apart the steps in a plan are.

One time scale. Every step in the plan is one environment step. To look 40 steps ahead, the planner samples a full 40-action sequence, rolls the model forward 40 times, scores it, and repeats for hundreds of candidates. The search grows with the horizon, so looking further ahead gets expensive fast.

Two time scales. We plan twice, at different resolutions. The coarse model jumps 8 steps at a time, so a 40-step future is only 5 decisions: roughly where should the block be at step 8, 16, 24, 32, and 40. Then the fine model fills each gap with real actions. Five short searches instead of one long one.

Because k is the only difference, setting k = 1 turns our method back into plain CEM. That gives us a control we can check against the baseline.

One point to keep straight: the two models are the same size and see the same data. The difference is what they predict. The fine model learns "state now, action, state one step later." The coarse model learns "state now, then state 8 steps later." Two time scales is about prediction distance, not model capacity.

03

What we are adding

A two-speed world model

Two GRUs over the same latent representation. The fine one predicts the next state one step ahead. The coarse one predicts the state k steps ahead, with k at 4 or 8. The coarse model is not trained on separate data, just on the same trajectories with a longer stride.

A coarse-to-fine solver

This is the main contribution, and it slots into the repo's solver interface next to CEM:

  • Sample candidate waypoint sequences with the coarse model, score them, keep the best.
  • For each gap between chosen waypoints, use the fine model to find the actions that get there.
  • Execute the first few actions, then re-plan.

Plain CEM does the second step only, across the whole horizon, which is why it needs so many samples to look far ahead.

Two measurements nobody has run here

  • Planning budget. Success rate against compute. Our claim is that hierarchy wins when the budget is small.
  • Generalization. Train on the default PushT appearance, evaluate across the 16 factors of variation without retraining, and compare against the repo's DINO-WM reproduction, which uses a frozen image encoder.
04

What we are not doing

Writing this down now saves an argument in week 3.

  • No new environment, and no environment other than PushT.
  • No new dataset format.
  • No attempt to beat state of the art on PushT success rate.
  • No claim that our GRU is a better world model in general. We claim the planner benefits from time abstraction.

Requirements

Setup

  • Python 3.10, installed from source with uv sync --extra all --group dev.
  • One pinned commit of stable-worldmodel, recorded in the README. Nobody upgrades mid-project.
  • A shared $STABLEWM_HOME so we are not each downloading the data.
  • One shared evaluation config. Horizon, receding horizon, episode count, and seeds are set once and never edited per person.

Data

PushT collected once, fixed seed, Lance format. Every model trains on that same dataset. Nobody retrains on their own collection run.

06

Experiments

Every result uses at least three seeds and reports mean and spread.

A. Planning budget

Our model with fine only against fine plus coarse, planned with CEM and with our solver, at roughly four budgets (50, 100, 300, 600 samples). Output: one line chart, success rate against budget.

B. Generalization

Our model against the DINO-WM baseline, evaluated on the default appearance plus the 16 factors of variation. Output: one table or bar chart, success rate per factor.

C. Control

Set k = 1. Our solver should now behave like CEM and score like it. If it does not, the solver has a bug or an unfair advantage. This check is not optional and it runs before the other two.

What counts as success

The project succeeds if we can state a clear result, including a negative one. Experiment C passes, A and B produce numbers with error bars, and anyone can rerun any number from the README in one command. "Hierarchy did not help" is a valid finding and we will report it. A number we cannot reproduce or explain is not.

Four weeks

  • Week 1Two people collect PushT data and train the fine model until it plans at all with stock CEM. Two people reproduce the DINO-WM baseline and record its numbers. One person pins the commit, writes the shared eval config, and sets up the results folder and plotting script.
  • Week 2Train the coarse model. Write the solver. Run Experiment C, and do not move on until it passes.
  • Week 3Run Experiments A and B across seeds. Fill in charts as results land, not at the end.
  • Week 4Draft the report. Code freezes Monday: bug fixes and documentation only after that.
