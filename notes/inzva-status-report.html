01

What we are testing

We are testing whether planning at two time scales beats planning at one when the compute budget is small. A flat planner searches every step of a 40-step horizon. Ours plans five coarse waypoints at a stride of eight, then fills in between them. If we are right, the hierarchy reaches the same success rate with far fewer model evaluations, and the gap widens as the budget shrinks.

We compare three systems under one evaluation protocol, holding everything else fixed.

Experiment C, which we run first

We set the stride to one, and the hierarchy then has to collapse to row 2's score. We treat this as a correctness check on our implementation rather than a result, and it costs us almost nothing. We run it before A and B, because if it fails neither of them means anything.

Experiment A

We compare rows 2 and 3 across compute budgets. This is our actual claim, and the only one that has to succeed.

Experiment B

We compare rows 1 and 3 across factors of variation, as evidence of generality. We drop this one if compute runs short.

02

What we have done

We locked the protocol. One shared config fixes episode count, budget, goal offset, resolution and seeds. Changing it silently invalidates every comparison we report, so we agreed that nobody edits it alone.

We closed the reproduction question. We could not reproduce the published 96%, and we tested and eliminated every mechanical explanation for it: the solver's sampling change, the success criterion, the block geometry, the environment itself, every numeric planning parameter, and the dataset format. We settled on 87.3% with a spread of 7.6 points over three seeds as our baseline of record.

We ran the same three seeds on three machines, spanning three GPU generations, three CUDA builds and two operating systems.

No pair disagrees by more than two episodes in 150, and all three means land within 0.6 points. We had predicted that per-seed rates would drift further apart on different hardware, and we were wrong: the three-seed mean is portable.

We got the cluster working. We wrote job scripts and a shared environment file, and added a processor-only preflight that catches a broken setup in about a minute instead of after a half-day queue.

We locked the model decisions: a stride of 8, a horizon of 40, one action per planning step, 224 pixels everywhere, and a single encoder shared between the coarse and fine models, then frozen.

04

What we decided about DINO-WM

For Experiment B we need a number from the published DINO-WM checkpoint. We found and fixed three separate faults in getting it to run, and scripted or configured all three rather than writing them down as steps to repeat by hand. It now loads and plans.

We have not scored it. We cancelled the evaluation for time. What we did learn is what it costs us.

Per environment, per planOne seed, 50 episodes
LeWMabout 1 second64 seconds
DINO-WM, RTX A4000282 seconds7.8 hours
DINO-WM, Tesla V100145 seconds4.0 hours

That puts three seeds at roughly twelve GPU-hours, against a cluster queue that has been quoting us more than a day. We see three options, cheapest first:

  • We run it on the cluster whenever the queue allows. If it scores near the published 74%, we need no training for Experiment B at all.
  • We drop Experiment B and report A and C only, since A is our actual claim.
  • We train DINO-WM ourselves. This is our most expensive route, and we only take it if the checkpoint scores poorly and we decide B is essential.

We decided this does not block anything. It sits off our critical path, so we start it when the queue is free and carry on writing the method in the meantime.
