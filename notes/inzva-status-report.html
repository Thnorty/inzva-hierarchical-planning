01

What we are testing

Planning at two time scales beats planning at one, when the compute budget is small. A flat planner searches every step of a 40-step horizon. Ours plans five coarse waypoints at a stride of eight, then fills in between them. If the claim holds, the hierarchy reaches the same success rate with far fewer model evaluations, and the gap widens as the budget shrinks.

Three systems, one evaluation protocol, everything else held fixed.

Experiment C, first

Experiment A

Rows 2 against 3 across compute budgets. This is the actual claim, and the only one that must succeed.

02

What is finished

The protocol is locked. One shared config fixes episode count, budget, goal offset, resolution and seeds. Changing it silently invalidates every comparison in the report, so nobody edits it alone.

The reproduction question is closed. The published 96% is not reproducible. Every mechanical explanation was tested and eliminated: the solver's sampling change, the success criterion, the block geometry, the environment itself, every numeric planning parameter, and the dataset format. Our baseline is 87.3% with a spread of 7.6 points over three seeds, and that is what we compare against.

Three machines agree. The same three seeds, on three GPU generations, three CUDA builds and two operating systems.

No pair disagrees by more than two episodes in 150, and all three means land within 0.6 points. That replaced a prediction that per-seed rates would drift further apart on different hardware. They do not. The three-seed mean is portable.

The cluster works. Job scripts, a shared environment file, and a processor-only preflight that catches a broken setup in about a minute instead of after a half-day queue.

The model decisions are locked: a stride of 8, a horizon of 40, one action per planning step, 224 pixels everywhere, and a single encoder shared between the coarse and fine models, then frozen.

04

The DINO-WM decision

Experiment B needs a number from the published DINO-WM checkpoint. It now loads and runs, after three separate faults were found and fixed. All three are scripted or configured rather than written down as steps to repeat by hand.

It has never been scored. The evaluation was cancelled for time. What we do know is what it costs.

Per environment, per planOne seed, 50 episodes
LeWMabout 1 second64 seconds
DINO-WM, RTX A4000282 seconds7.8 hours
DINO-WM, Tesla V100145 seconds4.0 hours

So roughly twelve GPU-hours for three seeds, and the cluster queue has been quoting more than a day. Three options, cheapest first:

  • Run it on the cluster whenever the queue allows. If it scores near the published 74%, Experiment B needs no training at all.
  • Drop Experiment B and report A and C only. A is the actual claim.
  • Train DINO-WM ourselves. The most expensive route, justified only if the checkpoint scores poorly and B is considered essential.

Do not let this block anything. It is independent of the critical path. Start it when the queue is free and carry on writing the method.

05

Things that will bite you

Each of these cost hours to find. All are documented in full in the repo manual.

TrapWhat it does
Seed noise is 14 pointsAt three seeds, any effect under about 5 points is indistinguishable from luck. Experiment A has to clear that bar, or show its gap widening consistently across budgets
The frozen shared encoderIt is trained for short-horizon prediction, but the coarse model needs features that predict eight steps ahead. If A shows no benefit at any budget while C passes, suspect this first
The cluster needs a non-default torch buildThe standard package has no kernels for its GPUs. It installs cleanly and then fails on first use
DINO-WM is 145 times slower than LeWMBudget for it in days, not afternoons
A refused pull leaves the cluster on old codeJob submission keeps working regardless, so runs look healthy while executing stale code. Verify the pull landed before submitting
