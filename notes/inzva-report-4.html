01

What we are testing

We are testing whether planning at two time scales beats planning at one when the compute budget is small. A flat planner searches every step of the horizon. Ours plans five coarse waypoints at a stride of two, then fills in between them. If we are right, the hierarchy reaches the same success rate with far fewer model evaluations, and the gap widens as the budget shrinks.

We compare three systems under one evaluation protocol, holding everything else fixed.

We compare rows 2 and 3 across compute budgets. This is our actual claim, and the only one that has to succeed.

We compare rows 1 and 3 across factors of variation, as evidence of generality. We drop this one if compute runs short.

02

What we have done

We locked the protocol. One shared config fixes episode count, budget, goal offset, resolution and seeds. Changing it silently invalidates every comparison we report, so we agreed that nobody edits it alone.

We closed the reproduction question. We could not reproduce the published 96%, and we tested and eliminated every mechanical explanation for it: the solver's sampling change, the success criterion, the block geometry, the environment itself, every numeric planning parameter, and the dataset format. We settled on 87.3% with a spread of 7.6 points over three seeds as our baseline of record.

We ran the same three seeds on three machines, spanning three GPU generations, three CUDA builds and two operating systems.

No pair disagrees by more than two episodes in 150, and all three means land within 0.6 points. We had predicted that per-seed rates would drift further apart on different hardware, and we were wrong: the three-seed mean is portable.

We got the cluster working. We wrote job scripts and a shared environment file, and added a processor-only preflight that catches a broken setup in about a minute instead of after a half-day queue.

We locked the model decisions: a stride of 8, a horizon of 40, one action per planning step, 224 pixels everywhere, and a single encoder shared between the coarse and fine models, then frozen.

What we decided about DINO-WM

For Experiment B we need a number from the published DINO-WM checkpoint. We found and fixed three separate faults in getting it to run, and scripted or configured all three rather than writing them down as steps to repeat by hand.

We scored it at 84.0% over three seeds, under exactly the protocol we use for LeWM. We raced the same evaluation across an RTX A4000 and two cluster GPU partitions, and kept whichever finished first.

That is ten points above the 74% most often cited for this same protocol, and inside the 84% to 92% band that independent reruns report. Against our LeWM baseline of 87.3% it is 3.3 points lower, which at three seeds sits inside seed noise, so we treat the two as indistinguishable.

We decided to use this checkpoint as our Experiment B reference, and not to train DINO-WM ourselves. One caveat travels with the number: the checkpoint comes from a fork of the harness and is marked epoch 10, so we report it as this checkpoint rather than as DINO-WM in general.

It is expensive to evaluate, which limits how often we can afford to rerun it.

05

What we decided: the planning horizon

Before the robot moves, the planner imagines what would happen if it took some sequence of actions. The horizon is how many steps ahead it imagines. We locked 40 because our method needs it: 40 steps split into 5 waypoints of 8 steps each. That number came from our design, not from testing how well anything plans with it.

It aims past the target. In evaluation the planner is shown the state 25 steps ahead and told to reach it, and a plan is scored only on where it ends up on its last imagined step. At a horizon of 40 the planner is therefore working to be in the right place 15 steps after the goal. LeWM never had this problem: its horizon is exactly 25 steps.

Two further effects push the same way. The model’s predictions drift the further ahead it imagines, and the planner searches a much larger space of action sequences with the same effort.

Horizon51015202540
Success, one seed50%60%40%22%24%14%

Over three seeds our model scores 15.3% at a horizon of 40 and 62.0% at 10, the latter pooled over two runs of three seeds (58, 62, 60 and 64, 70, 58). Fixing a second setting, how often the planner replans, later took it to 69.3%.

Those two runs used the same model, config and seeds, and differ by 8 episodes in 150, where a LeWM rerun moves by 1 or 2. So our planner is noisier run to run than the reference, and a single three-seed run here is worth less than it looks. We briefly read the first run as unusually steady; the second run showed that was luck.

Experiment A is unfair in our own favour until we settle this. If the flat planner is pinned at a horizon we have measured as bad, beating it proves little. A fair comparison puts the hierarchy against the best flat setting.

The encouraging reading is the opposite one: that collapse from 60% to 14% as the horizon grows is exactly the weakness the hierarchy claims to fix, and we have now measured it on our own model.

Decided: horizon 10, stride 2, still five waypoints. The method is unchanged; only the gaps between waypoints shrink, from 8 steps to 2. We rejected sweeping the horizon as a second axis of Experiment A on compute rather than on merit, and it remains the most informative version if the budget allows.

06

Experiment C passed, and a first look at A

Experiment C is the control the spec calls mandatory. Set the stride to one, so our planner has no abstraction left to exploit, and it must score like the ordinary planner. It does: 70.0% against 69.3%, four episodes apart out of 150 and inside our measured rerun noise. Our contribution is not winning through a bug or an unfair advantage.

With that cleared, here is one point of Experiment A, at the default compute budget. All four use the same protocol, the same lookahead and the same replanning interval.

Ordinary planner, fine model72, 74, 6269.3%

We beat the ordinary planner by about 7 points, and the control says that gap is real.

We do not beat planning coarsely and never refining. Both land on exactly 76.0%. At this budget our whole gain comes from searching 5 coarse decisions instead of 10 fine ones, and the refinement stage adds nothing we can measure.

If that survives the budget sweep, our honest headline is that time abstraction helps and refinement does not. That is a result worth reporting, and it is why coarse-only planning now belongs in Experiment A as a row of its own.

What this is not: the claim we are testing is about small budgets, and 300 samples over a 20-dimensional search does not starve the ordinary planner. One point is not a curve. The sweep is where this gets settled, and it is the last thing we have to build.
