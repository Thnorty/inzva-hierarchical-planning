Planning at two time scales

Does a world-model planner do better when it picks waypoints first and fills in the actions after? We built one, and we took apart a published one.

Our project asks one question: does a planner that thinks at two time scales beat one that thinks at one? We answered it two ways. We built our own hierarchical planner, and we dissected the published one.

The idea

Imagine first, then act. Hierarchy imagines in bigger steps.

A world model predicts what an action will do. The planner, CEM, samples action sequences, imagines each one, keeps the best tenth and repeats. Every extra step of lookahead makes that search bigger and lets the model's errors compound.

Push-T: push a T-shaped block onto its target. The pusher can only push, never grasp.

The top row is the usual flat planner: ten decisions, searched together. The bottom row is the hierarchical idea: choose five waypoints two steps apart, then fill in the actions between them. Five short searches instead of one long one.

Part 1 · A setting we never measured

Our first planning horizon came from the design. It cost us 47 points.

Why 40 fails

The goal is 25 steps away, and a plan is scored only on its last imagined step. At 40 the planner aims to be in the right place 15 steps after the goal.

Three seeds

Horizon 40: 15.3%. Horizon 10: 62.0%, pooled over two runs. Fixing how often it replans later took it to 69.3%.

INZVA_README.md §13 · notes/results/gru_horizon40_results.md · inzva_gru_results.md
We chose a 40-step horizon because 40 divides neatly into five waypoints. Nobody had measured it. On one seed, success falls from 60 percent at horizon 10 to 14 at 40. The reason: the goal is 25 steps ahead, and a plan is scored on where it ends, so at 40 the planner is aiming 15 steps past the goal. Over three seeds, 15 percent became 62. We locked horizon 10 and stride 2: still five waypoints, just closer together.

Part 1 · Experiment A, the budget sweep

Bigger steps win most when compute is scarce. Refining adds nothing.

Same compute

The coarse model beats flat CEM by +12.0 points at 15,000 evaluations per plan (p = 0.0003) and +9.6 at 7,500. Flat CEM stalls near 67% even at four times the compute.

Refinement

Ours and the coarse model alone differ by at most 2.4 points at every budget, never significantly.

Control (Experiment C) passed first: with the stride set to 1, ours scores 70.0% against flat CEM's 69.3%.

notes/results/expA_sweep_results.md · 5 seeds × 50 episodes per point · paired McNemar · RTX A4000 · expC_results.md
Experiment C came first: with the stride set to one our planner must behave exactly like CEM, and it does, 70.0 against 69.3. Then the real experiment: we swept the planning budget for three planners, five seeds each. On the x axis is compute, how many times the model is called per plan. Planning with the coarse model, the dashed line, sits above flat CEM everywhere, and at equal compute it is 12 points ahead where compute is scarce. Flat CEM stalls around 67 percent. But our full planner, the green line, lies right on top of the coarse model alone. The refinement step adds nothing at any budget. So time abstraction pays, most when compute is scarce, and our refinement does not.

Part 2 · Reproducing Hi-LeWM

The paper's planner reproduces. Its two fixes do not.

Part two. Hi-LeWM adds a high level that proposes subgoals for the low-level planner. We ran the paper's own checkpoints at its own budget. Plain Hi-LeWM reproduces: 36 against 38.7. Feeding it the expert's subgoals reproduces too: 70 against 73. But the paper's two fixes, constraining the search to real data, online and staged, give 34 and 46 where the paper reports 49 and 64. The 64 sits outside our interval.

Why are the subgoals bad? The high-level search finds plans that the model scores nine and a half times better than the expert's own actions. Nothing real is better than the expert, so the planner is mining the model's mistakes. Real macro-actions use only five to seven of their 32 dimensions; the search walks into the empty ones. Decoded, plain CEM's subgoals often smear the T into a grey cloud. The constrained fix, Hi-LeWM-C, keeps the block recognisable and its subgoals three and a half times closer, but success does not move. That is the result we did not expect.

Both parts taught us the same thing. Our own horizon, chosen for neat arithmetic, cost us 47 points. The paper's shipped config sets the low-level horizon to 5 while its authors ran 2, and with expert subgoals that difference alone is 32 points. Settings copied from a design or a config file are measurements nobody has made yet.

What we take away

Thinking in bigger steps helps. Good subgoals are the hard part.

  1. Part 1Planning in bigger steps beats flat CEM, most when compute is scarce: +12 points at equal compute. Refining between the steps, our own addition, adds nothing at any budget.
  2. Part 2Hi-LeWM's high level is the bottleneck. Its subgoals cost 22 points, because the search exploits the model. Keeping it on real data fixes the subgoals but not the success rate.
  3. BothMeasure every inherited setting. Two horizons nobody had measured were worth 47 and 32 points.

Three things to remember. In our planner, thinking in bigger steps beats flat CEM, by twelve points at equal compute when compute is scarce; refining between the steps adds nothing.
